Reason 1 of 6

Which channels actually bring in students?

The channel that gets the credit in your reports is often the one that closed the decision, not the one that opened it. A family decides over months: a radio spot, a billboard near home, a friend’s opinion, an open day, and finally a search for the school’s name. Last-click rules give that final search 100% of the credit. Blake, Nosko and Tadelis tested a version of this at eBay: they switched off paid search ads on brand queries in part of the market and found that nearly all the lost clicks and sales were recovered through unpaid search, so the paid brand clicks had no measurable short-term benefit. eBay is a household name and a school is not, so test before assuming the same, but the lesson holds: searches for your name mostly capture demand that was created somewhere else.

A precise report is not the same as a correct one

Platform dashboards give clean, precise numbers that can still be far from the truth. Gordon, Zettelmeyer, Bhargava and Chapsky compared observational measurement methods with randomized experiments in 15 Facebook advertising tests published in Marketing Science. They found that the observational methods often failed to reproduce the experimental results, even after controlling for extensive demographic and behavioral data. The reason is intuitive for admissions: the families who click your ad are already more interested in your school than the ones who do not. The Wanamaker complaint, whoever said it first (Quote Investigator’s history of the line), has lasted because the problem has.

What leaves no click is often what is working. Radio, billboards, fairs and open days cannot be followed, so a click-based report shows them contributing nothing. Cutting them then looks free, until the effect shows up weeks later in the search volume and the applications, as the next demo shows. The long-run effect of advertising is larger than the short-run one: Sethuraman, Tellis and Briesch put the average long-term elasticity at 0.24 against 0.12 short-term, so judging a channel by the month it ran is judging it by half of its effect.

What MMM does differently

Marketing Mix Modeling looks at the relationship between spend and results over time, not at individual clicks. It uses weekly or monthly totals: what you spent in each channel and how many leads, applications and enrollments followed. From those it estimates what each channel contributes, what would have happened anyway, and the return per peso. Google’s Meridian and Meta’s Robyn are both open-source implementations, and Robyn’s documentation highlights exploring adstock rates and saturation curves, the two ideas that make the method fit real advertising. Meridian also lets you calibrate the model with experiment results, so the numbers are checked against something other than the model itself.

You need less data than most teams expect. In general terms: spend by channel and period; leads, applications and enrollments for the same periods; and ideally two or more admissions cycles and at least three channels. Google presents Meridian as measurement that meets “marketers where they are”, and the same spirit applies here: if the data is incomplete, the audit evaluates what is usable instead of rejecting you. The next question, once you know what each channel contributes, is where you are already spending more than it returns.
