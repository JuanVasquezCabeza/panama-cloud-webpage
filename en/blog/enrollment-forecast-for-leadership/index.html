Reason 4 of 6

An enrollment forecast leadership can use

Leadership asks for a number, and the honest answer is a range with a probability. Rectors, CFOs and boards ask the same question every year: will we reach the intake target, and what does it take? Marketing usually answers with activity (campaigns, impressions, leads) instead of the number that matters. The pressure is real: Gartner’s 2025 CMO survey found 59% of CMOs saying their budget was too small to execute their strategy, and its research lead warned that flat budgets may face in-year cuts. A forecast with a range is how you defend a budget under that pressure.

Leads are not enrollments, and last year’s conversion rate says little about next year’s mix. A pipeline report shows what is already in the funnel, not what a different budget would do to it. By the time the funnel looks thin, there is little time left to react. Costs also move: in RNL’s reports, the median cost to recruit an undergraduate at US private four-year institutions went from $2,114 in 2020 to $2,795 in 2022, up 32%. An assumption that cost per enrollment stays put is exactly the one that breaks.

What a forecast from the model gives you

Because the model links spend by channel to enrollments, any budget scenario becomes a forecast with a range. Given the approved budget, how many enrollments should we expect, and how far is that from the target? If we are short, how much more closes the gap, and in which channels? If the budget is cut, how much intake are we giving up? Try it below: set the target and the budget, and read the probability of reaching it. The numbers are illustrative.

A forecast is most valuable when it arrives early. Marketing effects take time: in the Sethuraman, Tellis and Briesch meta-analysis the long-term advertising elasticity (0.24) was double the short-term one (0.12). So signals appear months before intake closes, and a forecast refreshed with each month of results shows whether the cycle is on track while there is still time to change the mix. Move through the cycle below and notice how the range narrows while the window to act closes.

What a forecast is not

A forecast is a calibrated estimate, not a guarantee, and presenting it that way makes it more credible, not less. Measurement errors are real, even at the biggest platforms, as Gordon and colleagues showed in 15 Facebook experiments. A range with a stated probability tells a board how sure you are, and it improves as more admissions cycles are added. Small experiments, such as Google’s geo experiments, can check the model where the decision is big. This is the end of the chain: contribution, saturation and allocation lead to a forecast leadership can approve, and its shape, with its peaks and valleys, comes from the seasonality of admissions.
