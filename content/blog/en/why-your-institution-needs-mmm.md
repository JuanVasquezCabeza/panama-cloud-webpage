**Most educational institutions cannot say which part of their admissions budget brings students, and that is a measurement problem, not a marketing problem.** The line “half the money I spend on advertising is wasted, but I don’t know which half” has been pinned on the retailer John Wanamaker for a century, although [Quote Investigator could not confirm he ever said it](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2022/04/11/advertising/). Pressure comes from the budget side too: [Gartner’s 2025 CMO survey](https://secure.businesswire.com/news/home/20250512782208/en/Gartner-2025-CMO-Spend-Survey-Reveals-Marketing-Budgets-Have-Flatlined-at-7.7-of-Overall-Company-Revenue) found marketing budgets flat at 7.7% of company revenue, with 59% of CMOs saying their budget was too small to execute their strategy. And each student is getting more expensive to win: in [RNL’s 2022 cost-of-recruiting report](https://newamerica.org/documents/9177/RNL_2022_CostRecruiting_Report.pdf), the median cost to recruit one undergraduate at US private four-year institutions rose from $2,114 in 2020 to $2,795. When every enrollment costs more and budgets do not grow, knowing which channel works stops being optional.

**Marketing Mix Modeling answers that question with data you already have, without following a single family.** It relates what you spent in each channel each week or month to the leads, applications and enrollments that followed, while accounting for seasonality, delayed effects and saturation. Google describes its open-source [Meridian](https://developers.google.com/meridian) as built to enable “privacy-durable, advanced measurement”, and Meta’s [Robyn](https://github.com/facebookexperimental/Robyn) presents modern MMM as “a privacy-safe solution”, so two of the biggest ad platforms now publish MMM tooling themselves. The idea is not new: Simon Broadbent introduced “adstock”, the lingering effect of advertising, in [a 1979 paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advertising_adstock). What changed is that tracking people got harder while the computing got cheap.

## Why it matters for an educational institution

**An enrollment is worth years of tuition, so a small efficiency gain is worth a lot.** A student who stays six years turns every extra enrollment into a long stream of tuition. That is why even a modest improvement in the mix can justify real analysis, far more than a business that sells once. RNL’s figures show how much institutions already pay per student, and the same logic applies in Latin America, with different numbers (the RNL data is from the US, so use it as a sign of how the cost is built, not as a benchmark). **Admissions has traits that generic marketing models ignore.** There is one main decision window per year, channels work for months before a family asks, and a large share of the mix is traditional media that leaves no click. Advertising effects also last: in a meta-analysis of 56 studies, [Sethuraman, Tellis and Briesch (2011)](https://www.gwern.net/doc/economics/advertising/2011-sethuraman.pdf) estimated an average short-term advertising elasticity of 0.12 and a long-term one of 0.24, about double. A report that only looks at the same month will therefore undervalue slow channels. Binet and Field’s analysis of 996 campaigns in the IPA databank, [The Long and the Short of It](https://ipa.co.uk/media/14463/long_and_short_of_it_presentation_final_030424.pdf), found the best results when brand building and activation were balanced, around 60:40, although [Mark Ritson cautions](https://www.marketingweek.com/ritson-brand-building-boost-short-term-sales/) that this is an average across very different situations.

## The six reasons

**The six reasons are one chain, not six separate benefits.** First you learn which channels contribute, then where each one stops paying, then how to split the budget, and finally how to defend that split with a forecast leadership can use. Two more reasons explain why the traditional methods fail and how a model corrects for it: measuring without tracking people, and the admissions cycle. ## Where to start

**You do not need perfect data to start; the first step is finding out what you have.** That is the marketing budget and data audit, which ends with a recommendation and an implementation plan. It also looks at where a small experiment could check the model, because [Gordon and colleagues](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2019/a-comparison-of-approaches-to-advertising-measurement-evidence-from/) found, across 15 Facebook experiments, that observational methods often failed to reproduce what randomized experiments showed. [Google’s geo experiments](https://research.google/pubs/measuring-ad-effectiveness-using-geo-experiments/) randomly assign geographic regions to test or control conditions, and Meridian lets you [calibrate models with experiments](https://developers.google.com/meridian). You decide with evidence, not with a vendor’s promise.
