Rectors, CFOs and boards ask a simple question every year: **will we reach the intake target, and what does it take?** Marketing usually answers with activity (campaigns, impressions, leads), not with the number that matters: how many students will enroll.

## Why the usual answer falls short

Leads are not enrollments, and last year’s conversion rate says nothing about what happens when the mix of channels changes. A pipeline report can tell you what is already in the funnel, but not what a different budget would do to it, and by the time the funnel looks thin there is little time left to react.

## What a forecast from the model gives you

Because the model links spend by channel to enrollments, any budget scenario can be turned into an **expected enrollment forecast**, with a range:

- Given the approved budget, how many enrollments should we expect, and how far from the target is that?
- If we are short, how much additional budget closes the gap, and in which channels?
- If the budget is cut, how much intake are we giving up?

> Illustrative example, not data from any client. The target is 600 new students. With the planned budget the model expects between 540 and 610, most likely 575. Leadership sees that the target is at risk and asks what closes the gap: the model shows that moving part of the budget and adding a modest amount would bring the likely value to 600.

## Early warning, not just a report

Since marketing effects take time (see the guide on the admissions cycle), a forecast updated with each month of results shows months ahead whether the intake is on track. That is time to adjust the mix while it still changes the outcome.

## What a forecast is not

It is not a guarantee. It is a calibrated estimate with a range, and it improves as more cycles of data are added. Presenting it that way makes it more credible with a board, not less.

## Where this connects

This is the end of the chain: knowing what each channel contributes, where it saturates, and how to split the budget leads to a forecast that leadership can approve. The shape of that forecast, with its peaks and valleys, comes from the seasonality of admissions, explained in the guide on the admissions cycle.
