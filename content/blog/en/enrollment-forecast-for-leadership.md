**Leadership asks for a number, and the honest answer is a range with a probability.** Rectors, CFOs and boards ask the same question every year: will we reach the intake target, and what does it take? Marketing usually answers with activity (campaigns, impressions, leads) instead of the number that matters. The pressure is real: [Gartner’s 2025 CMO survey](https://secure.businesswire.com/news/home/20250512782208/en/Gartner-2025-CMO-Spend-Survey-Reveals-Marketing-Budgets-Have-Flatlined-at-7.7-of-Overall-Company-Revenue) found 59% of CMOs saying their budget was too small to execute their strategy, and its research lead warned that flat budgets may face in-year cuts. A forecast with a range is how you defend a budget under that pressure.

**Leads are not enrollments, and last year’s conversion rate says little about next year’s mix.** A pipeline report shows what is already in the funnel, not what a different budget would do to it. By the time the funnel looks thin, there is little time left to react. Costs also move: in [RNL’s reports](https://newamerica.org/documents/9177/RNL_2022_CostRecruiting_Report.pdf), the median cost to recruit an undergraduate at US private four-year institutions went from $2,114 in 2020 to $2,795 in 2022, up 32%. An assumption that cost per enrollment stays put is exactly the one that breaks.

## What a forecast is not

**A forecast is a calibrated estimate, not a guarantee, and presenting it that way makes it more credible, not less.** Measurement errors are real, even at the biggest platforms, as [Gordon and colleagues](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2019/a-comparison-of-approaches-to-advertising-measurement-evidence-from/) showed in 15 Facebook experiments. A range with a stated probability tells a board how sure you are, and it improves as more admissions cycles are added. Small experiments, such as [Google’s geo experiments](https://research.google/pubs/measuring-ad-effectiveness-using-geo-experiments/), can check the model where the decision is big. This is the end of the chain: contribution, saturation and allocation lead to a forecast leadership can approve, and its shape, with its peaks and valleys, comes from the seasonality of admissions.
